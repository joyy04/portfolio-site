LA History

A concept mapping tool for learning city history. It teaches Los Angeles history across four eras through an interactive map, quizzes, and a concept mapping tool paired with a Socratic AI tutor, grounded in Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development.

my role
UX design and user research. I wrote the test protocol and ran the sessions.

tools
Figma, Cytoscape, Flask, Ollama

One round of changes shipped. Second round not yet tested.
the challenge!

Students read history passively, so get them to build the connections themselves, with a tutor that scaffolds thinking instead of handing over answers.

Research

I wrote the protocol and ran moderated usability sessions with four participants using a think aloud method.

Onboarding friction. Most participants skipped or skimmed the tutorial, then got stuck later.

The AI tutor was not used as designed. Meant to be a Socratic scaffold, it was mostly ignored or treated like a generic chatbot with short, low effort questions.

“The instructions make sense to you guys because you made them.”Participant 3, exit interview

Design response

onboarding

before

A static popup.

after

A spotlighted walkthrough that dims the screen and points at each feature directly.

AI tutor scope

before

Open ended Q&A.

after

A tool bound to the concept map. It responds to the student's current nodes and prompts revisions instead of answering unrelated questions.

Outcome

One round of testing led to these changes, but there was no second round to confirm they actually worked. Next step: retest the onboarding and tutor scope with new participants.

The Green Bean

Ordering ahead for a campus cafe with no digital line. The cafe had no way to order ahead, and baristas had to stop taking orders at peak hours.

  • No ordering channel besides standing in line, no matter the wait.
  • Across 22 student interviews, this was the most consistently cited frustration.

Design decisions

Ordering ahead with a live queue position. Mocked up as a UI element, not backend logic, since this was scoped as a design project. The mechanism: prep time per drink times orders ahead in line, updating in real time. I chose a moving number over a flat "10 minutes" because the real question people had was not how long, it was where they stood.

Club fundraiser drinks. Came from a request from MENASA's president, who was running an Arab American Heritage Month fundraiser. I generalized the one time ask into a standing home page feature any club could use going forward.

Testing and iteration

I walked 12 students through the Figma prototype before finalizing the design.

what worked

The fundraiser section resonated strongly, validating it as a standing feature rather than a one time add.

queue position label

before

after

Reworded so it reads as a live position. Follow up walkthroughs showed the confusion was resolved.

Outcome

Completed as a course project, not deployed at the cafe. Next step: pilot with cafe staff to validate the queue based estimate against real prep times.

Posture monitoring

Scoring sitting and standing posture in real time from a 12-sensor wearable. This is my senior comprehensive project for Computer Science. I'm the only researcher on it, working with my advisor, Professor Camarillo.

my role
Sole researcher and student investigator
advisor
Professor Camarillo
tools
Sony Mocopi sensors, Unity
status
In progress
the question!

Can a wearable score sitting and standing posture in real time using joint angles and fixed thresholds, without machine learning?

acceptable range sensor points torso outside it
12sensors
~20participants
2rounds of testing

Approach

A Sony Mocopi wearable with 12 sensors streams body motion into Unity. The system reads the angles between body segments and scores them against thresholds for sitting and standing posture. Scoring from angles keeps every score traceable to a measurement.

Setting the threshold. I start from angle ranges reported in the literature. In the first round of user testing, participants sit as straight as possible, then slouch as much as possible. Those two extremes, across the group, set one fixed threshold for the target population, instead of calibrating live for each participant.

The study

About 20 participants, tested in two rounds.

  1. 1

    Human subjects approval

    The study goes through Occidental's human subjects review first. I'm writing the proposal and consent form.

  2. 2

    Round one

    Collect the sit-straight and slouch extremes, and use them to set the threshold.

  3. 3

    Refine the model

    Update the scoring from what round one shows.

  4. 4

    Round two

    Test the refined model with participants. Targeted for the end of October.
