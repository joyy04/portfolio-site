About

Two majors, one question: why do people do what they do on screen?

I study Computer Science and Cognitive Science at Occidental College, which means most of my design decisions get made twice: once for whether they work, and once for whether they match how people naturally think, remember, and make choices.

In practice that looks like running actual user testing before I commit to a direction, being willing to throw out a feature idea when research contradicts it, and being specific about the mechanism behind a design decision rather than just the decision itself.

The two case studies below cover an educational history app built around Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development, and a campus cafe ordering app built from 22 student interviews.

Case study 01

LA History: a concept mapping tool for learning city history

Role: UX design & user research Team of 3 · Occidental College Figma · Cytoscape · Flask · Ollama
  • Problem
  • Research
  • Design response
  • Outcome
Problem
Students read history passively instead of building real understanding of it.
My role
UX design and user research, team of 3
Outcome
One round of changes shipped, second round not yet tested

Problem

LA History teaches Los Angeles history across four eras through an interactive map, quizzes, and a concept mapping tool paired with a Socratic AI tutor, grounded in Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development.

  • History apps default to passive reading: see a fact, move on.
  • The goal was an experience where students build connections themselves, with a tutor that scaffolds thinking instead of handing over answers.

Research

I wrote the protocol and ran moderated usability sessions with four participants using a think aloud method.

Finding 1: Onboarding friction

Most participants skipped or skimmed the tutorial, then got stuck later.

"The instructions make sense to you guys because you made them." Participant 3, exit interview
Finding 2: The AI tutor was not used as designed

Meant to be a Socratic scaffold, it was mostly ignored or treated like a generic chatbot with short, low effort questions.

Design response

  • Onboarding: replaced the static popup with a spotlighted walkthrough that dims the screen and points at each feature directly.
  • AI tutor scope: narrowed the chat from open ended Q&A to a tool bound to the concept map, so it responds to the student's current nodes and prompts revisions instead of answering unrelated questions.

Outcome

One round of testing led to these changes, but there was no second round to confirm they actually worked. Next step: retest the onboarding and tutor scope with new participants.

Case study 02

The Green Bean: ordering ahead for a campus cafe with no digital line

Role: UX/UI design Client: Occidental College cafe Figma, 22 interviews, usability test with 12 students
  • Problem
  • Design decisions
  • Testing & iteration
  • Outcome
Problem
No way to order ahead, baristas had to stop taking orders at peak hours
My role
UX/UI design, 22 interviews, usability test with 12 students
Outcome
Completed as a course project, not deployed at the cafe

Problem

  • Peak hour lines got long enough that baristas had to stop taking orders entirely.
  • No ordering channel besides standing in line, no matter the wait.
  • Across 22 student interviews, this was the most consistently cited frustration.

Design decisions

Ordering ahead with a live queue position. Mocked up as a UI element, not backend logic, since this was scoped as a design project. The mechanism: prep time per drink times orders ahead in line, updating in real time. I chose a moving number over a flat "10 minutes" because the real question people had was not how long, it was where they stood.

Club fundraiser drinks. Came from a request from MENASA's president, who was running an Arab American Heritage Month fundraiser. I generalized the one time ask into a standing home page feature any club could use going forward.

Testing & iteration

I walked 12 students through the Figma prototype before finalizing the design.

What worked

The fundraiser section resonated strongly, validating it as a standing feature rather than a one time add.

What didn't

The queue position was confusing at first. I revised the label text so it reads as a live position, not a fixed wait time. Follow up walkthroughs showed the confusion was resolved.

View the Figma prototype

Outcome

Completed as a course project, not deployed at the cafe. Next step: pilot with cafe staff to validate the queue based estimate against real prep times.
